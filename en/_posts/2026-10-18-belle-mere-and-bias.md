When my *belle-mère* helps me understand AI's linguistic bias.

We understand intellectually what this bias covers: models are trained on languages that do not always express things in the same way.

But truly making the idea your own is harder.<br>
So I looked for a simple example, an everyday word that could reveal how different languages express the same reality in very different ways.

That word is *belle-mère*.

In French, a *belle-mère* is both your spouse's mother and your father's new wife.<br>
English keeps them apart: your spouse's mother is your mother-in-law, your father's new wife is your stepmother.

Where French merges, English distinguishes.

In Russian, Korean or Chinese, the word changes depending on whether she is the husband's mother or the wife's mother.<br>
In Tamil, a mother-in-law can be called by the same term as an aunt, reflecting a tradition in which marriages were often made between families that were already related.

And yet the people being named are the same.

French adds an ambiguous mark of affection or courtesy: the *belle*, "beautiful", in *belle-mère*.

English emphasises either a bond born of a contract, her child's marriage ("in-law"), or a loss: "stepmother" comes from the root *steop*, which marked the loss of a parent.

Other languages emphasise which side of the family she comes from. Tamil recalls a kinship that already existed.

None of these views is more accurate than another. They simply reveal that languages do not merely express the world differently.

They choose what to bring to the fore.

And this is where linguistic bias takes on its full meaning. To translate *ma belle-mère* into English, an AI has to choose between "mother-in-law" and "stepmother". If the French text does not say which, it guesses, based on what its training data mostly contained. And it will most often guess the same way.

Reality itself has not changed.

What we retain is what language chooses to make visible, and what it leaves in the shadows.
