Teams building their own agents without coordination. Processes fragmenting. Validation steps scattering.

AI is sold as a technology that breaks down silos. Without governance, it does exactly the opposite: it re-silos functions.

With an added difficulty: these partitions are hard to inventory. How many agents are actually running, who built them, which data they rely on, what happens when two of them contradict each other — few organisations have the answer today.

That is why governance has to be designed before deployment.

Four questions seem to me to come first.

Who owns the subject<br>
An AI initiative does not progress durably without a senior voice that validates proposals, arbitrates priorities and sets the phasing. I hold that role for my own scope. What it actually costs is unpopular arbitration: saying no to a use case a team believed in, delaying a deployment because the data is not ready, or catching a use case that skipped who reviews the output. Without sponsorship, projects move forward — each in its own direction.

Who answers for what<br>
Who designs the agents? Who validates them? Who supervises them? Who answers when something goes wrong? Without a written answer, agents become orphans: impossible to maintain, impossible to embed, and above all impossible to defend in front of an auditor.

Who needs to be in the room<br>
Data, IT, quality, business. An agent designed without one of those four functions eventually collides with it — on data quality, technical integration, validation, or the reality of the process it is meant to serve. That is how a silo forms: not through bad intent, but because something that needed to connect was built alone.

What happens next<br>
This is the most neglected point. An agent is not a static script: it evolves, it learns, it interacts with systems that change. In a regulated environment, that means versioning, activity logs, full traceability — and retirement criteria.<br>
A system that evolves has to have its changes documented — what changed, when, and why.

That reflex already exists in our profession; it now has to be applied to objects that learn.

Governance is not a brake. It is what separates the organisation that takes pride in how many agents it has from the one that knows what these agents do — and why each of them is there.

How many agents are running in your organisation today — and who could tell you with certainty?
